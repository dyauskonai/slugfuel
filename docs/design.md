# Design direction

## Product priorities

Help a student decide what to eat quickly on a phone. The dashboard should emphasize today's progress and the next useful action. Menus and comparisons should remain accessible without lengthy onboarding.

## Initial visual direction

Use a warm neutral background, dark readable text, and one restrained green accent. Start with one readable font family, a small consistent type scale, and spacing based on a shared scale. These are starting choices, not a completed visual identity.

Give hall and meal names the strongest hierarchy, then foods and portions, then detailed nutrition. Keep nutrient order and units consistent. Align numbers so comparisons are easy.

## Screens to design

- Dashboard: selected date, goals, consumed totals, remaining amounts, and logged meals.
- Menus: date and meal controls, hall selection, and food details.
- Plate builder: foods, serving adjustments, total nutrition, and log confirmation.
- Recommendations: several plates, their halls, match explanations, and swaps.
- Favorites: saved foods and dated menu appearances.

## Interaction details

Use clear labels, visible focus states, sufficient contrast, and generous touch targets. Do not encode nutrients or status through color alone. Respect reduced-motion preferences. Motion should explain state changes, such as recalculated totals.

Design loading, empty, stale, unavailable, and no-match states alongside successful results. Handle long food names and incomplete nutrition information. Avoid fake metrics, fabricated food photography, or decorative elements that obscure the decision.

## Validate with students

Ask a student to find a meal, adjust a portion, and log it without coaching. Record where they hesitate and fix the largest sources of confusion before adding visual effects.
